SEOUL, April 1946 — New National Museum Celebrates Everyday Korean Life

Amid the joy of liberation, the National Museum of Ethnology has opened beneath Namsan in Seoul to present the Korean people's everyday culture. Folklorist Song Seok-ha, who collected material across the country during Japanese rule, began leading preparations last November, soon after liberation. Most of the exhibits come from his long-held personal collection.

The galleries display farming tools, clothing, pottery, masks and ornaments once used in daily life. Song stressed that cultural heritage extends beyond royal treasures to the ordinary household goods that carry a people's spirit. Efforts to preserve customs threatened by Japan's assimilation policies have finally borne fruit.

Visitors paused before familiar old objects they had not seen for years. An elderly man grew tearful at a spinning wheel like the one his grandmother used, calling the sight dreamlike. Students examined traditional dance masks with curiosity.

The museum plans to collect and display more folklore material from across the country. Limited funding and staff in the difficult conditions after liberation pose major challenges. Even so, the opening is widely welcomed as a first step toward recording and preserving Korean everyday culture through Korean hands.
